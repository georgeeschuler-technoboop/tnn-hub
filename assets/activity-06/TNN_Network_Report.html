Each respondent named and rated their working relationships across five dimensions: trust, energy, support, creativity, and how well they know the other person's work. The picture that emerges is not a popularity ranking. It is closer to a map of where effort already flows, and where it does not yet.

Density alone does not say whether a network is healthy. It says whether the wiring is there for things to move through it. At 0.229, the wiring is there.

What density tells us

0.05–0.10 is typical for a distributed network. 0.10–0.20 marks an active, well-maintained one. This network sits at 0.229. Above 0.50 starts to look like a tight team rather than a network at all.

§06
Rhythm

The relationships are real. The rhythm is the open question.

Among the connections people described, 87% happen monthly or less. Only 16 out of 123 are weekly or more. And 56% are roughly balanced; the rest lean more in one direction than the other.

Worth trying

A thirty-minute monthly peer call. A shared channel with one standing prompt. A quarterly pairing check-in. Any of these would activate trust and energy that already exist between gatherings, without asking anyone to add a major commitment.

§12
Pattern

Mostly learning together. Building together is rarer.

  • Peer learning and knowledge exchange is the most common connection type, by a wide margin.
  • Informal support and thought partnership follows closely behind. A lot of these relationships are sounding boards.
  • Joint programming or co-delivery is meaningfully rarer; the clearest room to grow.

Referrals, funding, and governance ties are present but small. These tend to be the highest-trust, highest-commitment relationship types, which is exactly why there is headroom here.

Thinking together is the foundation that building together gets built on. The network appears to be exactly where that sequence would predict.

§13
Next

Four moves worth making

1. Protect the people holding it together

Stacy Brungardt, Tim Soerens, Kristina Fruge, and Keri Burns carry a disproportionate share of the connective load. Ask each of them directly what would make it easier, or how that load could be shared more widely.

2. Run the aspired-connections list

People have already named who they want to build with. A facilitated pairing session could turn that list into real working relationships in one gathering.

3. Build a light rhythm between gatherings

A monthly call or a channel with a standing prompt would activate trust and energy that's currently dormant between convenings.

4. Make two warm introductions

A small, specific effort to connect Alyssa Ruch and Ashley Wilson into the rest of the network closes the one clear gap this data shows.
